Feedback from students:

* Friday 5pm is a bit of a rush job because recitations end at 3:30, so only
  have 90 minutes to finish homework after the last contact. Proposal: make
  homework due at midnight Sunday. This way people have the freedom to work on
  it over the weekend, while still not overlapping new content that happens on
  Monday.
* Desperately need more coverage of git and editors (VS Code)
* They really did like p5 and want to keep using it, and they're aware that the
  p5 web editor is training wheels.
* Practice is important. Current homeworks are heavy, and there's nothing to
  ladder them up. So having more little exercises would help.
* Continuity is also important. We're learning this topic because it lets you do
  _this_, and _this_ lets you learn the next topic, which lets you do _that_,
  and so on.
* Preparation is important too: read X and complete Y before the first content
  lecture. This way there is already a landing spot for what is covered, and
  forces people to stay engaged.
* The really difficult homework can be frustrating but that also translates into
  a sense of satisfaction for having done it. My job here is to give people
  everything they need to be able to take on difficult homework.

Possibly good idea: incremental coding exercises where the student works through
a series of tasks that build on one another. First, they see a running p5 sketch
of a final piece. Then, throughout the section, they are asked to build parts of
it.

Stupid example:

* we're going to build >this< crane game (shows running thing, no code)
* first, make the ground and the sky (picture)
* then do some more lesson text
* second, make two rectangles for the crane vertical and armature
* more text
* third, add a grabber thing that responds to keyboard input
* etc

Each segment is optional, but the following segment will be pre-supplied with a
solution that students can refer to.

I like the idea of giving students an unfolding set of steps that can show them
that they can build complex things. The head trip here is that the main
ingredient to building complex things is decomposition. What do you see? How
could it work? Can we break that apart into several smaller pieces? And once you
have the small pieces, building it up is just a matter of tenacity.
