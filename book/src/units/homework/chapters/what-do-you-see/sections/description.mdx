## Rough notes: photo identifications

### snail-shell.jpg

<ZoomImage src={snailShell} alt="A snail shell" width={300} />

- Most likely an apple snail, _Pomacea canaliculata_ (channeled apple snail).
- The shell is a logarithmic spiral, but the growth ratio is not the golden
  ratio.
- Student idea: measure the whorl expansion rate from the photo (width ratio of
  successive whorls along a line through the apex) and fit r = a·e^(bθ).
- 3D extension: Raup's shell coiling model generates the shape from three
  parameters: whorl expansion rate, distance from the axis, and translation
  along the axis.

### dendrobium.jpg

<ZoomImage src={dendrobium} alt="A dendrobium plant" width={300} />

- Leaves grow on alternating sides at predictable intervals (alternate
  phyllotaxis, distichous).
- Here the leaves look equidistant, but that isn't always so in similar-looking
  plants.
- Leaf size varies, possibly in a predictable way.

### dahlia.jpg

<ZoomImage src={dahlia} alt="A dahlia flower head" width={300} />

- A dahlia in the cactus or semi-cactus form (_Dahlia_ hybrid, family
  Asteraceae).
- Each "petal" is an individual flower, called a ray floret. The whole head is a
  colony of them.
- Math: concentric layers, where each "petal" sits distance d from the center at
  angle theta. Good use of rotate-then-translate, like turtle graphics.
- Color gradient runs from yellow to pink.
- No two petals are identical. Add variation with random numbers or noise.

- Coconut palm, _Cocos nucifera_ (Hawaiian: niu).
- Same scene taken about 12 hours apart. A study in the geometry _and_ the
  passage of time, for an animated result.
- One of the "canoe plants" the original Polynesian voyagers brought with them.
- 20-25ish fronds grow out of the crown in various directions, looped all around
  like the dahlia.
- Gravity pulls each frond downward.
- The trunk is a smooth curve with a bend: a good candidate for a Bézier curve.
- Wind for an animated result: the fronds sway, and the entire tree sways to a
  lesser degree, driven by a randomly changing wind source.

### hibiscus.jpg

<ZoomImage src={hibiscus} alt="A Chinese hibiscus flower" width={300} />

- Chinese hibiscus, _Hibiscus rosa-sinensis_. Hawaiian word for hibiscus:
  aloalo. The state flower is a (different) hibiscus.
- Petals are arranged in a pentaradial pattern (the zoologists' term, from
  echinoderms like starfish) or, near-synonymously, a pentamerous one (the
  botanists' term, "five parts", often written 5-merous). Pentaradial stresses
  the five-fold radial symmetry; pentamerous stresses the parts coming in fives.
- Color changes as a function of distance from the center.
- Petals overlap like a pinwheel. A quick web search should turn up more obvious
  photos of this.
- How might you model the ruffly outer edge of the petals?
- Stamens: where they emerge from the staminal column jutting out of the flower,
  how long they are, the angle they grow out at, and the little yellow
  pollen-producing bits at the end of each.
- The "micro-flower" at the tip of the column isn't a flower. It's the female
  part: five fuzzy red pads called stigmas.
- The pentaradial configuration is everywhere on this flower.

### noni.jpg

<ZoomImage src={noni} alt="Noni fruit and leaves" width={300} />

- Noni, _Morinda citrifolia_ (Hawaiian: noni; also called Indian mulberry).
- The photo shows fruit with "eyes". Each polygon on the surface was at one
  point a flower, and they fused together to form the fruit.
- The little fruit still has a flower visible.
- The leaves are glossy and green, with an alternating pattern of veins.
- Another canoe plant.

### bird-of-paradise.jpg

<ZoomImage src={birdOfParadise} alt="A bird of paradise flower" width={300} />

- Bird of paradise, _Strelitzia reginae_ (also called crane flower).
- Native to South Africa, and related to bananas. A common ornamental in Hawaii.
- So much math going on here! No rotational symmetry, but a regular pattern in
  how the orange parts grow out.
- A decent approximation is possible using triangles and quads.
- Colors: green, red, orange, purple.
- Stretch goal: find other images of this in different states of growth, and
  create an animation that shows the flower growing.

### lace-fern-maybe.jpg

<ZoomImage src={laceFern} alt="A fern frond" width={300} />

- Unsure if this is exactly palapalai, _Microlepia strigosa_ (lace fern), but it
  is certainly some kind of fern.
- A single long vein from which leaves emerge, in what looks like an alternating
  pattern.
- Each leaf is composed of smaller leaflets.
- Leaf size: longer toward the base, tapering to small leaves at the tip. The
  overall leaf length cuts a nice curve as well.
- The self-similarity lends itself to recursion, or even recursive fractals. Two
  well-known options: the Barnsley fern (an iterated function system that plots
  a fern from random points) and L-systems (Lindenmayer systems, which grow a
  plant by repeatedly rewriting a string of drawing commands).

### wart-fern.jpg

<ZoomImage src={wartFern} alt="A wart fern frond" width={300} />

- Laua&#x02BB;e, _Microsorum grossum_ (synonym _Phymatosorus grossus_; often
  labeled _M. scolopendria_; also called wart fern or maile-scented fern).
- Not all examples show this pattern of lobes. In this picture the lobes seem to
  emit from the central vein, but that isn't always the case.
- Along each lobe's vein, the dots are positioned nearby, usually
  non-overlapping, but you can see examples of overlap. The dots are sori
  (singular: sorus), clusters of spore-producing structures.
- The thing I love about this one is the irregularity: it seems to follow a
  general plan, but with lots of random variation.

### lobster-claw.jpg

<ZoomImage
  src={lobsterClaw}
  alt="A hanging lobster claw heliconia"
  width={300}
/>

- Hanging lobster claw, _Heliconia rostrata_. A South American plant, and one of
  Bolivia's national flowers.
- Fun words to describe this one: a pendant chain of red _bracts_ that abruptly
  change from red towards the main chain to yellow at the tips.
- Each bract is slightly smaller than the one above it (maybe 90%).
- Each bract is self-similar, so creating a `drawBract` function makes sense.
- The overall structure grows _downward_ with gravity.
- What can you say about how large the bottom-most bract is? How might you use
  that as a stopping condition for rendering these?

### pink-quill.jpg

<ZoomImage src={pinkQuill} alt="A pink quill bromeliad" width={300} />

- Pink quill, _Wallisia cyanea_ (long known as _Tillandsia cyanea_, and still
  usually sold under that name). Commonly found in Ecuador, and a relative of
  pineapple.
- This plant also features bracts, but these grow upwards, alternating
  left/right in a herringbone pattern. Each bract's angle and size is somewhat
  less than the previous one.
- The color shifts in a lovely gradient from green at the bottom to pink at the
  top.
- The flower isn't quite legible in this photo, but if you wanted to include it
  I'm sure you can find examples with a google search. It has three petals.

### gardenia.jpg

<ZoomImage src={gardenia} alt="A gardenia flower" width={300} />

- A gardenia, most likely tiare, _Gardenia taitensis_ (Tahitian gardenia).
- The radial petal pattern is cool. This one has seven petals, but that number
  is variable. You'd get the angular offset by using `360 / n` for `n` petals.
- Each petal overlaps the one next to it slightly, and each petal seems to have
  a slight tilt where it veers off in a consistent direction, which reminds me
  of a circular saw blade or a propeller. Good use for Bézier curves.
- There's a little stigma (female part) in the middle.

### volcano.jpg

<ZoomImage src={volcano} alt={"Makana ridge above Hāʻena"} width={300} />

- Makana ridge above H&amacr;&#x02BB;ena, taken from Limahuli Garden.
- Included as an example of a 1D boundary function: the various ridges of the
  volcano have been shaped by wind and water over millions of years.
- If this was a volcano from the Big Island of Hawai&#x02BB;i, it would be a
  fairly flat dome. But this is quite spiky and makes for a more dynamic
  function.
- I do not think you can find a parametric function to approximate this, but
  instead will need to look into noise functions.
- You could model this using a 1-dimensional noise function, and allow the curve
  to rise or fall based on what that noise function says.
- You might even combine different noise functions: one to determine the overall
  peaks/valleys, and another to determine the bumpiness between them.
