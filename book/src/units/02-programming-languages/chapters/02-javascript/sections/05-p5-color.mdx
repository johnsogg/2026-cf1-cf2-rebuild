# Color

Eyes are amazing. When electromagnetic waves enter your eye and stimulate the
cones in your retina, you 'see' the result of biological computations that give
you information about color and brightness. Your mind puts this together such
that you effortlessly see a tree, the sky, or whatever else you're looking at.
Our eyes sense frequency ranges, and our minds see color.

When you see a picture of a tree/sky/whatever on a computer, you're processing
the light from millions of tiny picture elements ("pixels") usually composed of
microscopic red, green, and blue dots. The pixel light that reaches your eye is
actually nothing like what a real tree reflects, but your vision system
interprets it in the same way.

For graphics programming, our job is to make those pixels glow with the right
balance of red/green/blue to produce the color that we intend the user to see.

## Pixels and Colors

The physical pixels on your display are probably made up of red, green, and blue
sub-pixels. But in code, we can model color in lots of different ways. RGB is
one of the most common: you just record how much red, green, and blue comprise a
color, and those components combine to form a high-fidelity color.

For example, the color purple has equal parts red and blue, without green.

Importantly, "how much" of these components is bounded. You can have zero green,
but you can't have less than that. Similarly there's an upper bound, you can
think of it as being 100%, and you can't go above that in a meaningful way.
Numerically, we just pick a convention for what that range is, and write down
colors using that convention.

If our convention was percentages, purple is 31% red, 0% green, and 31% blue.
Often the conventional range is from zero to one, so our purple is 0.31 red, 0
green, 0.31 blue. And other times we use a conventional range of integers from 0
to 255 (inclusive), in which case our purple is 80 red, 0 green, 80 blue.

### Color Models

For code to work with colors, we need to pick a strategy. I introduced the RGB
approach above, and there are many
[color models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_model). Practically, you've
probably experienced this when you've used color pickers:

These are all subtly different. At left is our 80/0/80 purple color in the RGB
color space. The next two show the same color, but modeled in HSB (hue,
saturation, balance), and HSL (hue, saturation, luminance). The color models
give you different knobs to turn. They're useful in many contexts, but at this
point it would be distracting to dive deeper.

Regardless of which model you use, colors are translated into a format that can
be displayed in pixels, and any of these models can produce the same output.

### Named Colors

Using numbers to represent color is helpful because you can do math with it. But
it is also awkward to think with (what color is `55, 27, 7`?) so we can use
names for a limited set of colors (for example, `"saddlebrown"`).

Color on the web has gone through an evolution over the years. The "CSS Color
Module Level 4" (what a mouthful) is the current standard that can be directly
used by browsers. The WC3's spec has a
[formal list of every named color](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/#named-colors),
along with a note telling you that you shouldn't use them. And for production
work, they're right. But as you're just learning to code, go ahead and draw from
that list to your heart's content. Just be aware that there is weirdness, like
dark gray actually being lighter than gray.
