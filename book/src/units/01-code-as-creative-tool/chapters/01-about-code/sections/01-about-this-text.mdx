# About Computational Foundations

This is a book for turning creative ideas into code. There's a mystique
surrounding code, though: many people believe that it is deeply mathematical,
analytical, detail-oriented, and only for the true brainiacs.

While it is true that writing code _can_ be mathematical, and it pays for
programmers to be _analytical_ and _detail-oriented_, the same can be said of
music, oil painting, and architecture. What these activities all have in common
is that they are creative endeavors.

This book is for creative people - artists, mechanics, designers, inventors,
engineers. You'll learn to understand your big ideas as things that can be
modeled with software, and you'll learn to break apart big gnarly problems into
smaller, manageable pieces.

Code is not just a way of telling a computer what to do. Of course it _is_ that,
but it is _so much more_. Writing code is for clarifying thought. For telling
stories. For making an argument. For having fun. For connecting people.

#### Idea: _Tell Me Your Secrets!_

For me, programming is a way to better understand my own mind. You've probably
had an idea - vague, visual, definitely _probably_ awesome - and a nagging sense
that it is probably also kinda wrong, possibly impossible. Once you can code,
those weirdly compelling ideas will have another way to come into the world.
That vague idea, with all its wrong or confused parts, will clarify as you put
your brain to the idea, asking: what are you? how do you work? how are your
parts related? Is this a special case of something bigger?

## How This Book Is Structured

This book is for giving creative people another tool for thinking, for building,
for making arguments in artifact form. As with any tool, you must learn to use
it. The book starts from scratch, and incrementally adds new concepts. There
will be loads of examples, exercises, and jokes that fall flat.

My main purpose in writing all this is to support students in my Computational
Foundation courses at the University of Colorado in the ATLAS Institute. Of
course, anyone is welcome to use this book! But my focus is to support the
Creative Technology Design (CTD) students that I teach.

The first half or so is what you'd find in many introductory programming
classes: modeling things with data, getting user input, and doing something
useful or at least amusing. This will be heavy on interactive graphics.

The second half is about data structures and algorithms. If the first part was
about getting the high level overview of code, the second part takes us way down
into the dirty details.
