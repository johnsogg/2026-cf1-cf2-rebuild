# Language

This is a book about using programming languages. I'd like to start with a brief
diversion about what we really mean by _language_, because it will help you make
sense of what is to come. This chapter introduces some concepts - syntax,
semantics, idioms, and domain jargon - with direct parallels to programming
languages.

I'd bet that spoken language was the original disruptive technology. We can make
funny noises through our mouths and other people not only _hear_ it but also
_understand_ it. And then you can have a _dialog_ to talk about food, shelter,
and gossip. That first 'language' startup must have absolutely crushed it in the
Paleolithic.

## Syntax

For language to work, you need an agreed upon system. Words represent ideas:
nouns for things and concepts, verbs for actions, then you have adjectives,
adverbs, pronouns, prepositions, to support richer expressions. But we need more
than just a dictionary of words and their meanings. We need structure. We need
grammar.

Words can't come in an arbitrary order. Don't believe me?

> Arbitrary order an words come can't in. Me believe don't?

Grammar gives rules for how to string together words. This assists both speakers
and listeners. If you hear a grammatically damaged statement, it might consume
extra brain power to understand what you're hearing. Grammar is an umbrella term
for several related concepts including morphology (the form a word comes in),
phonology (what a word sounds like), and syntax (the sequential ordering of
words).

For the next few sections, we're going to focus heavily on _syntax_, because
programming languages don't have much in the way of morphology or phonology, but
are all about syntax.

## Semantics

The meaning of individual words can have a big impact on how a statement is
interpreted. Consider these two sentences with shape `The Noun1 Verb the Noun2`:

> 1. The man dressed the wound.
> 2. The man dressed the mannequin.

They have the same grammatical shape, but the _meaning_ (or _semantics_) is
different. The word 'dressed' is ambiguous until you can determine the object:
it has multiple interpretations, and until you have more information, any of
them are possible. Once you hear 'wound', you know which sense of 'dressed' was
intended.

Poetry, lyrics, and possibly terms of service documents often use ambiguity on
purpose for artistic or deceptive purposes. But when clear communication is
needed, ambiguity is the enemy.

Consider this actual (or maybe apocryphal?) headline:

> "British Left Waffles on Falkland Islands"

Unless you have context or a knowledge of history, the obvious reading of this
is absurd, and will send your brain into higher gear to figure out what it is
trying to say.

This was allegedly a 1982 headline from the Guardian (UK). The Brits didn't
forget their breakfast. The headline means that Brits who are politically
liberal are having a hard time making a decision.

## Idioms

An idiom is a cultural saying that shortcuts a certain idea, but isn't derivable
from the words themselves. Idioms are like inside jokes: if you are part of the
"in" group that understands how semantics are compressed into a word or phrase.
Consider these phrases:

> 1. Off base.
> 2. Right off the bat.
> 3. Ballpark figure.

If you're American, you're probably so accustomed to phrases like these that
they seem like plain English. But the moment you need to communicate with other
audiences, you might get puzzled looks. And that makes sense, because these
phrases are all
[baseball idioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_English-language_idioms_derived_from_baseball),
as are many, many American idioms.

So if you're writing for an international audience, my advice is to avoid
idioms. But if you're with culturally similar people, using idioms and slang can
make for more efficient communication.

## Domain Jargon

Like idioms, domain jargon is a manner of how language is _used_. This goes past
simple turns of phrase: domain-centric jargon can seem like foreign languages
themselves.

You're probably familiar with the term _legalese_ to describe the uptight mode
that legal documents are written, bristling with strange terms like _habeas
corpus_ or _tort reform_ or _amicus brief_. Jazz musicians talk about playing
_charts_, where they might _trade fours_, and eventually _go back to the head_.
To the uninitiated, the language is hard to understand, and can often seem like
the people using such jargon are doing it on purpose to exclude you. (And
sometimes that's true!)

Of course to a lawyer or a jazzer, these terms have exact meanings that are
wrapped up in a particular culture that facilitates more effective (or
exclusive?) conversation. If you go deep on any intellectual topic you will
certainly encounter jargon, and you will doubtlessly start using it yourself!
Jargon is one way that practitioners identify one another.
