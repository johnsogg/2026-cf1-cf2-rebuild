> Intro: interactive programs constantly ask spatial questions. Did the user
> click on this shape? Did the asteroid hit the ship? How far is the boid from
> its neighbor? This section covers the basic geometric primitives and the
> handful of tools for asking questions about them. Goal is knowing what tools
> exist and when to reach for them, not deriving formulas from scratch.

## Points

> A point is an (x, y) pair. Everything else in this section is built from
> points.

> Representing a point in code: two loose variables vs. an object `{ x, y }` vs.
> a `Point` class (callback to the classes section). Pick one convention for the
> rest of the section.

## Lines

> Two points define a line. Stub for how we represent one in code (a pair of
> points).

> Flag that "line" is doing a lot of work here; we'll get precise about it later
> in the section. For now, think of the line through two points.

## Polygons

> A polygon is an ordered list of points, connected in order, with the last
> point connecting back to the first. Drawing one with `beginShape()` /
> `vertex()` / `endShape(CLOSE)`.

> Rectangles and triangles are just polygons with special shortcuts in p5.
> Circles aren't polygons, but they're simple primitives too: a center point and
> a radius.

## How primitives interact

> Most geometric questions are about how two primitives relate to each other.

## Lines, segments, and rays

> The nuance flagged earlier. A _line_ extends to infinity in both directions. A
> _segment_ has two definite endpoints. A _ray_ has one endpoint and extends to
> infinity in one direction.

> Why it matters: the line/line intersection above was for infinite lines. Two
> segments drawn on screen might not touch, even though the lines through them
> do. Same for point-to-line distance: the closest point on a segment might be
> one of its endpoints.

> Figure idea: the same two pairs of points drawn as lines, segments, and rays,
> showing that whether they intersect changes.

> Most things you draw are segments. Lines and rays are mostly useful as tools
> for computing things (e.g. the ray in the next section).

### Parametric form

> A different way to write down a line: start at point A and travel toward B. A
> parameter `t` says how far you've gone. `t = 0` is at A, `t = 1` is at B,
> `t = 0.5` is the midpoint. Good spot for LaTeX: $P(t) = A + t\,(B - A)$.

> In code, this is two lines: `x = ax + t * (bx - ax)` and the same for y.
> Callback: this is the same idea as `lerp()` (if lerp has come up already).

> The payoff: lines, segments, and rays are the same formula with different
> allowed values of `t`. Segment: `0 <= t <= 1`. Ray: `t >= 0`. Line: any `t`.

> Ask idea: a slider for `t` that moves a dot along a segment, and keeps going
> past the endpoints when `t` goes below 0 or above 1.

> Revisit point-to-segment distance: find the `t` of the closest point on the
> infinite line, clamp it to `[0, 1]`, and measure to that point. Clamping is
> exactly the "closest point might be an endpoint" case from above.

### Intersecting segments, parametrically

> Write both segments parametrically, with `t` for the first and `u` for the
> second, and solve for where they're equal: $A + t\,(B - A) = C + u\,(D - C)$.
> Give the solution; don't derive it. (It's two equations in two unknowns; uses
> the 2D cross product.)

> What you get back is more than a point. The values of `t` and `u` tell you how
> the segments relate:
>
> - Both `t` and `u` in `[0, 1]`: the segments cross, on the interior of both.
> - Only one in `[0, 1]`: the infinite lines cross on one segment but off the
>   end of the other.
> - Neither: the lines cross, but off the ends of both segments.
> - `t` or `u` exactly 0 or 1: the segments touch at an endpoint.
> - Denominator is zero: the lines are parallel, so no single `t`. A second
>   check tells you if they're also collinear (on the same line), in which case
>   they might overlap.

> The reusable function returns all of this (e.g. `{ point, t, u }`, or a result
> that says "parallel" or "collinear"), not just a point or `null`. Callers pick
> the case they care about: hit tests want "interior of both", ray casting wants
> `t >= 0` and `u` in `[0, 1]`.

> Note on floating point: "exactly 0" and "exactly parallel" rarely happen with
> real numbers, so compare against a small tolerance.

> Ask idea: drag the four endpoints and show `t`, `u`, and which case you're in,
> live.

## Curves and splines

> Straight segments only get you so far. p5 has two built-in families of smooth
> curves: Bézier curves (`bezier()`, `bezierVertex()`) and cardinal splines
> (`spline()`, `splineVertex()`). Goal of this section: remove the magic. Both
> are parametric, just like the segment above, and both are built from the same
> `lerp` idea.

> Note on p5 versions: the book uses p5 v2, where these are `spline()` /
> `splineVertex()`. Older tutorials online call them `curve()` /
> `curveVertex()`.

> CurvePlayground: make a new playground called curve playground that lets the
> user playground with Bezier curves and splines. For Bezier curves, it's
> important to show the distinction between the segment and points, which are
> definitely on the curve and the two internal control points, which specify the
> tangent to the Bezier curve at its end points. Students will have seen these
> before in graphic editors, but probably have never thought about what is
> actually going on mathematically so we need to show that the two internal
> control handles specify that tangent direction. For the Cardinal spline, we
> need to show the important property of control points necessarily falling on
> the resulting curve.

### Bézier curves

> A cubic Bézier has four points: two endpoints (often called anchors) and two
> control points (often called handles). Like the segment, it's parametric:
> `t = 0` is the first anchor, `t = 1` is the last.

> Removing the magic, de Casteljau's construction: lerp between each pair of
> neighboring points at the same `t` (three points), lerp between those (two
> points), lerp once more (one point). That last point is on the curve. Sweep
> `t` from 0 to 1 and you trace out the whole thing. It's just segments within
> segments.

> Ask idea: interactive figure with a `t` slider that shows the three levels of
> lerp lines and the point they produce, with draggable control points.
> (Candidate for a custom immerse component, like TransformPlayground.)

> What the control points mean: the curve starts at the first anchor heading
> toward the first handle, and arrives at the last anchor coming from the
> direction of the second handle. Further-out handles pull the curve harder in
> that direction. The handles generally aren't on the curve; they pull it rather
> than pin it.

> The whole curve stays inside the shape (convex hull) formed by its four
> points. Handy for quick hit-test rejection.

> Optional, for the math-curious: the closed-form polynomial (Bernstein form),
> $B(t) = (1-t)^3 P_0 + 3(1-t)^2 t\,P_1 + 3(1-t)t^2 P_2 + t^3 P_3$. It's the de
> Casteljau construction multiplied out.

> p5: `bezier(x1, y1, ..., x4, y4)` for one curve. In v2, `bezierVertex()` takes
> one point per call, three calls per cubic segment (after a starting
> `vertex()`). `bezierPoint()` / `bezierTangent()` evaluate the curve at a given
> `t`.

### Cardinal splines

> A cardinal spline is defined by a list of points, and the curve passes through
> every one of them. No off-curve handles.

> How it decides its direction at each point: the tangent at a point is parallel
> to the line between its two neighbors (figure idea: segment A→C drawn parallel
> to the tangent at B). A tightness setting scales how strong that tangent is.
> Tightness 0 is the common Catmull-Rom spline (p5's default); tightness 1 gives
> straight segments.

> Consequence: each piece of the curve between two points depends on four points
> (the two it connects plus one neighbor on each side). The first and last
> points have only one neighbor, so something special has to happen at the ends.
> p5 v2 passes through them by default (`splineProperty('ends', INCLUDE)`), or
> you can use them only as direction guides that aren't drawn (`EXCLUDE`, which
> matches old `curveVertex()` behavior).

> Removing the magic: each piece of a cardinal spline is a cubic Bézier in
> disguise. The handles are computed from the neighboring points. (Optional:
> show the conversion.)

> p5: `splineVertex()` inside `beginShape()` / `endShape()`,
> `splineProperty('tightness', ...)`, `splinePoint()` / `splineTangent()`.

### Control and continuity

> Perception of control. Bézier: handles are off the curve, which can feel
> indirect at first, but they give you direct control over direction and pull at
> each end. This is what the pen tool in Illustrator/Figma/Inkscape is.
> Cardinal: what you click is what you get, since every point is on the curve,
> but you can't set the direction at a point on its own. Moving one point also
> bends the pieces next to it, and the curve can overshoot between points.

> Continuity: when you chain curves together, does the joint look smooth? Define
> informally: connected (no gap), same direction (no corner), same speed.
> (Optional: the C0 / G1 / C1 names.)

> Chained Béziers: connected if each curve starts where the last one ended, but
> smooth only if the handles on either side of the joint line up with it
> (collinear). That's your job. Design tools enforce this with "smooth" vs.
> "corner" points.

> Cardinal splines: smooth through every interior point automatically, since the
> tangent there is computed from the same neighbors on both sides. You get
> smoothness for free, and give up the ability to make a sharp corner without
> tricks.

> Ask idea: the same set of points drawn as a chain of Béziers and as a cardinal
> spline; drag a point and watch which parts of each curve move.

## Hit tests

> A hit test asks whether two primitives intersect. Usually one of them is a
> point (the mouse), so the question becomes "is this point inside this shape?"

> Point in circle: `dist(px, py, cx, cy) < radius`.

> Point in rectangle: axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) check with four
> comparisons. Previews the data structures chapter.

> Point near a segment: point-to-segment distance < tolerance.

> Point in polygon: cast a ray from the point in any direction and count how
> many polygon edges it crosses. Odd means inside, even means outside. Nice
> payoff for rays.

> Ask idea: a sketch with several shapes that highlight when the mouse is over
> them.

> Bridge to the next section: all of these tests assume the shape and the mouse
> are in the same coordinate system. But a ship that's been moved, rotated, and
> scaled draws itself in its own local coordinates. To hit-test it, we need a
> way to move between coordinate systems. That's what affine transforms are for.

## Affine transforms

> Intro: anything you draw can have its own local coordinate system. So far
> every shape has been placed using the canvas's (world) coordinates. Affine
> transforms let a drawing live in its own frame of reference, and then get
> placed into the world.

### Drawing relative to your own origin

> The Magpie (from Space Junk) knows how to draw itself around its own (0, 0),
> which sits in the middle of its hull. The platform, the window, and every
> other part are computed relative to that origin.

> Why this is a big deal: the drawing code doesn't need to know or care where
> the ship is, which way it faces, or how big it is. It just does its thing in
> its own frame of reference. Cognitive win; laziness in the programmer-virtue
> sense.

> Figure/sketch: the Magpie translated and rotated, world axes in gray, object
> axes in blue.

### The three common transforms

> Translate: move by some dx and dy. Rotate: spin the coordinate system by an
> angle (in radians). Scale: stretch or squish, either uniformly or with
> separate x and y factors.

> Brief mention that others exist (skew/shear) but these three cover nearly
> everything.

> Note: because y points down on the canvas, a positive rotation looks
> clockwise. (Ties back to the earlier coordinate-system discussion.)

### The current matrix in p5

> p5 (like most graphics libraries) keeps a "current" transform that applies to
> everything you draw. Calling `translate(dx, dy)`, `rotate(angle)`, `scale(s)`
> / `scale(sx, sy)` combines a new transform with what's already there; it
> doesn't replace it.

> The current matrix is subject to `push()` / `pop()`, just like fill and
> stroke. `pop()` throws away everything since the matching `push()`.

### Order matters

> Transforms are applied in sequence, so translate-then-rotate is not the same
> as rotate-then-translate. Typical recipe for placing an object: translate to
> where it goes, rotate it to face the right way, scale it to the right size,
> then draw at (0, 0).

### Under the hood: matrices

> In 2D, a 3x3 matrix transforms points from one coordinate system into another.
> Each of translate/rotate/scale has its own matrix shape. You won't write these
> by hand in p5, but it's useful to know they're there.

$$
\text{Identity} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\text{Translate} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & t_x \\ 0 & 1 & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\text{Scale} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & s_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\text{Rotate} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

> The identity matrix is the "do nothing" default: transforming a point by it
> gives back the same point. It's what the current matrix starts as (and what
> the playground's empty stack means).

> Why "affine": these transforms preserve parallel lines. Parallel lines in
> object space are still parallel after translating, rotating, or scaling.

> Combining: multiplying two 3x3 matrices gives another 3x3 matrix, so any chain
> of transforms collapses into one matrix. That's what p5's current matrix is.

> Aside: 3D uses 4x4 matrices, and GPUs are built to crunch these fast.
