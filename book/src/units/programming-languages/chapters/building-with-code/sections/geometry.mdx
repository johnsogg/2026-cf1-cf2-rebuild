# Geometry

Interactive programs constantly ask spatial questions. Did the user click on
this shape? Did the asteroid hit the ship? How far is this Boid from its
neighbor? Every one of those is a geometry question.

This section covers the basic geometric building blocks, and the handful of
tools for asking questions about them. You don't need to derive any of the
formulas from scratch. The goal is to know what tools exist, what they do, and
when to reach for them.

## Angles

Angles show up all over graphics code: which way a ship is facing, how far to
turn it, which direction a Boid is flying. Mathematicians traditionally use the
Greek letter $\theta$ (theta) for an angle, so you'll see it a lot. In code it
usually gets spelled out as `theta` or `angle`.

p5 measures angles in _radians_, not degrees. A full turn is $2\pi$ radians
(about 6.283), so a half turn is $\pi$ and a quarter turn is $\pi / 2$. p5
has constants for the common ones: `TWO_PI`, `PI`, `HALF_PI`, and
`QUARTER_PI`. If you'd rather think in degrees, `radians(90)` converts degrees
to radians, and `degrees(HALF_PI)` goes the other way.

Two functions connect an angle to a direction. Imagine an arrow of length 1
pointing at angle $\theta$. Then `cos(theta)` is how far that arrow reaches
along x, and `sin(theta)` is how far it reaches along y. For a longer arrow,
multiply both by its length. You've already used this: it's how the planets in
the animation section move around their sun, and how each Boid in the classes
section turns its heading into a velocity.

Drag the arrow around below. The colored bars along the axes are the arrow's
"shadows" on x and y, and they're exactly `cos(theta)` and `sin(theta)` times
the arrow's length.

<AnglePlayground />

A few things to notice as you spin it:

- At $\theta = 0$, the arrow points straight along x, so cosine is 1 and sine
  is 0. A quarter turn later, it's the other way around.
- Cosine and sine are never bigger than 1 or smaller than -1. The arrow can
  only reach as far as its own length.
- The angle grows _clockwise_ on screen. That's because y points down in p5. In
  a math class, where y points up, the same angle would turn counterclockwise.

Now flip the toggle so that $\theta = 0$ points up. Measuring angles from +x
(pointing right) is the standard convention. It's what math classes use, and
it's what p5's `cos()`, `sin()`, and `rotate()` all assume. But it's not the
only way to reckon about angles. Compass headings start from north, and lots of
games treat "up" as the natural direction for a ship to face. When $\theta = 0$
points up, the roles of cosine and sine switch: x comes from `sin(theta)`, and
y comes from `-cos(theta)`. Neither convention is wrong. What matters is
knowing which one your code is using. Mixing them up is a classic bug: the
ship's picture points up, but it flies off to the right.

You can also go the other way, from a direction to an angle. Given how far
something is in x and y, `atan2(dy, dx)` gives you the angle it points at,
measured from +x. Note the order: y comes first. For example,
`atan2(mouseY - shipY, mouseX - shipX)` is the angle that points from the ship
toward the mouse.

## Points

A point is a pair of numbers: an x coordinate and a y coordinate. Everything
else in this section is built out of points.

You could store a point as two separate variables, like `shipX` and `shipY`.
That works for one point, but it gets clumsy fast: a triangle needs six
variables, and a function that works on two points needs four parameters. It's
much tidier to keep each point's x and y together in one value. We already have
a tool for that: the p5 vector you met in the arithmetic section.

```js
let a = createVector(40, 200)
let b = createVector(240, 50)

console.log(a.x, a.y) // 40 200
```

A vector made with `createVector` has `x` and `y` properties, so for our
purposes it's just a point with a few handy methods attached. Any function in
this section that takes a point would work just as well with a plain object like
`{ x: 40, y: 200 }`, since all it ever looks at is `x` and `y`.

## Lines

Two points define a line. In code, we can represent a line as a pair of points:

```js
let start = createVector(30, 200)
let end = createVector(170, 60)
line(start.x, start.y, end.x, end.y)
```

Fair warning: the word "line" is doing a lot of work here. The thing p5's
`line()` draws has two ends, but in math, a line goes on forever in both
directions. We'll get precise about this later in the section. For now, think of
"the line through two points."

## Polygons

A polygon is an ordered list of points, where each point connects to the next,
and the last point connects back to the first. In p5, you draw one by listing
its corners between `beginShape()` and `endShape(CLOSE)`. The `CLOSE` is what
connects the last point back to the first.

Rectangles and triangles are polygons too. p5's `rect()` and `triangle()` are
just shortcuts for drawing those common shapes. Circles aren't polygons, but
they're simple too: a circle is a center point and a radius.

## How primitives interact

Points, lines, polygons, and circles are _geometric primitives_. Most geometry
questions are about how two primitives relate to each other: how far apart they
are, whether they touch, and where.

### Distance between two points

The distance between two points comes from the Pythagorean theorem. The
difference in x (often written $\Delta x$, "delta x") and the difference in y
($\Delta y$) are the two legs of a right triangle. The distance is the
hypotenuse:

$$
d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}
$$

Drag the points around and watch the triangle and the numbers change. Notice
that $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ can be negative, but since they're squared, the
distance never is.

<PointPlayground />

p5 has this built in as `dist(x1, y1, x2, y2)`, and plain JavaScript has it too:
`Math.hypot(x2 - x1, y2 - y1)` gives the same answer. (`hypot` is short for
hypotenuse.) It's worth writing yourself once, though, because you'll see this
same calculation inside lots of other geometry code.

### Distance between a point and a line

The shortest path from a point to a line meets the line at a right angle. So the
distance from a point to a line is the length of the perpendicular from the
point to the line.

With A and B on the line and P the point, there's a compact formula for this.
It uses two vectors: $B - A$, which points along the line, and $P - A$, which
points from A to P. Divide the cross product of these two vectors by the length
of the line's vector:

$$
d = \frac{|(B - A) \times (P - A)|}{|B - A|}
$$

(If you want a refresher on vector subtraction and dot and cross products, see
the Vectors part of the Arithmetic section.)

You don't need to memorize this formula. Just know that it exists. One bonus:
without the absolute value bars, the cross product's sign tells you which side
of the line P is on.

But usually what we draw isn't an infinite line, it's a segment with two ends.
That changes things. If the perpendicular from P lands between A and B, it's
still the shortest path. If it lands past one of the ends, the closest point on
the segment is that end.

Drag P past the end of the segment and watch the closest point Q get stuck on
the endpoint. The dashed line shows the perpendicular to the infinite line,
which no longer reaches the segment.

<PointLinePlayground />

This is exactly the question behind "did the user click on this line?" Nobody
can click on a line that's only a pixel or two wide. Instead, we check whether
the distance from the mouse to the segment is less than a few pixels.

### Where two lines intersect

Two lines either cross at exactly one point, or they're parallel and never
cross. (Or they're the same line, and they "cross" everywhere.)

We'll define those properly in a moment, but you can probably guess from the
picture.

The readout treats each segment as a vector, like in the arithmetic section:
$r = B - A$ and $s = D - C$. Their products tell you a lot about the lines:

- The **cross product** $r \times s$ is zero exactly when the lines are
  parallel. That's how code checks for the "they never cross" case. Its sign
  matters too: it tells you which way you'd turn to get from $r$'s direction to
  $s$'s. In p5's y-down coordinates, a positive cross product means $s$ points
  clockwise from $r$ on the screen, and a negative one means counterclockwise.
- The **dot product** $r \cdot s$ is zero exactly when the lines are
  perpendicular.

The readout also shows the shortest distance between the two objects. Try making
two segments parallel, then pull them apart. Then turn them into lines and see
how the distance changes.

We could write a function that finds the intersection point of two lines, but
we're about to see a better way to describe lines that makes the function more
useful.

## Lines, segments, and rays

Now for the nuance we promised. There are three different things people call a
"line":

- A **line** goes on forever in both directions.
- A **segment** has two definite endpoints, and nothing beyond them.
- A **ray** has one definite starting point, and goes on forever in one
  direction.

Why does it matter? The intersection of two infinite lines might be nowhere near
the segments you've actually drawn on the screen. When you saw the hollow circle
in the playground above, the lines crossed, but the segments didn't. The same
goes for distance: the closest point on a segment might be one of its endpoints,
but a line has no endpoints.

Most of the things you draw are segments. Lines and rays are mostly useful as
tools for computing things. You'll see a ray do real work in the hit testing
section below.

### Parametric form

Here's another way to describe the line through A and B. Start at A, and travel
toward B. A number called a _parameter_, usually named `t`, says how far you've
gone. At `t = 0` you're at the starting point A. At `t = 1` you're at the other
point B. At `t = 0.5` you're halfway between them.

$$
P(t) = A + t\,(B - A)
$$

In code, that's one line for x and one for y:

```js
let x = a.x + t * (b.x - a.x)
let y = a.y + t * (b.y - a.y)
```

If that looks familiar, it should: it's lerp! You're mapping `t` from the range
0 to 1 onto the range from A to B. p5 even has a shortcut for doing this to
vectors, `p5.Vector.lerp(a, b, t)`.

Here's the payoff. Nothing stops `t` from going below 0 or above 1. Negative
values of `t` are behind A, and values above 1 are past B. So lines, segments,
and rays are all the same formula. The only difference is which values of `t`
you allow:

| Kind    | Allowed values of `t` |
| ------- | --------------------- |
| Segment | `0 <= t <= 1`         |
| Ray     | `t >= 0`              |
| Line    | any `t`               |

This playground is the same as the last one, with parameters turned on. The `t`
slider moves the point P(t) along the line through A and B. Slide it past 0 or
1, and switch the first object between segment, ray, and line. P(t) turns into a
hollow circle whenever the current `t` isn't on the object.

<LinePlayground showParameters />

Parametric form gives us a neat way to find the closest point on a segment.
First find the `t` of the closest point on the infinite line (the foot of the
perpendicular). Then _clamp_ it to the range 0 to 1, and plug it back into
$P(t)$. Clamping is exactly the "closest point might be an endpoint" case from
the point-to-line playground. If the perpendicular lands at `t = 1.4`, clamping
pulls it back to `t = 1`, which is B.

The `t` of the closest point on the infinite line through A and B is the dot
product of $P - A$ with $r = B - A$, divided by the dot product of $r$ with
itself:

$$
t = \frac{(P - A) \cdot r}{r \cdot r}
$$

That's a dense formula, so let's unpack it. Imagine shining a light straight
down onto the line, perpendicular to it, so P casts a shadow on the line. The
shadow lands at the closest point.

- $P - A$ is the arrow from A to P.
- $r \cdot r$ is the segment's length, squared. (Dot a vector with itself and
  you get `x * x + y * y`, which is the Pythagorean theorem without the square
  root.)
- $(P - A) \cdot r$ is how far P's shadow lands from A, multiplied by the
  segment's length. That comes from the $\|A\|\|B\| \cos \theta$ form of the
  dot product.

Dividing the second by the first leaves the shadow's distance from A divided by
the segment's length. That's the fraction of the way from A to B, which is
exactly what `t` means. In code:

```js
const r = p5.Vector.sub(b, a) // the arrow from A to B
const ap = p5.Vector.sub(p, a) // the arrow from A to P
const t = ap.dot(r) / r.dot(r)
```

Let's try it with numbers. Say A is `(10, 10)`, B is `(30, 10)`, and P is
`(15, 40)`. Then `r` is `(20, 0)` and `ap` is `(5, 30)`:

- `ap.dot(r)` is `5 * 20 + 30 * 0 = 100`
- `r.dot(r)` is `20 * 20 + 0 * 0 = 400`
- `t` is `100 / 400 = 0.25`

Plugging `t = 0.25` into $P(t)$ gives `(10 + 0.25 * 20, 10 + 0.25 * 0)`, which
is `(15, 10)`. That's on the segment, a quarter of the way from A to B, right
above P. Now move P to `(50, 40)`, past the end of the segment. This time `ap`
is `(40, 30)`, so `t` is `800 / 400 = 2`. Clamping pulls that back to `t = 1`,
and the closest point is B.

If your points are plain objects instead of p5 vectors, write the dot products
out by hand. For example, `ap.dot(r)` is
`(p.x - a.x) * (b.x - a.x) + (p.y - a.y) * (b.y - a.y)`.

### Intersecting segments, parametrically

To intersect two segments, write both of them in parametric form, using `t` for
the first and `u` for the second, and find where they're equal:

$$
A + t\,(B - A) = C + u\,(D - C)
$$

That's two equations (one for x, one for y) with two unknowns. With $r = B - A$
and $s = D - C$, the solution uses the 2D cross product:

$$
t = \frac{(C - A) \times s}{r \times s}
\qquad
u = \frac{(C - A) \times r}{r \times s}
$$

What you get back is more than just a point. The values of `t` and `u` tell you
how the two segments relate:

- If $r \times s$ is zero, you can't divide by it. The lines are parallel, and
  there's no single crossing point. (If they're also on the same line, they're
  called _collinear_, and they might overlap.)
- If `t` and `u` are both between 0 and 1, the segments cross, and the crossing
  is inside both of them.
- If only one of them is between 0 and 1, the infinite lines cross on one
  segment, but off the end of the other.
- If neither is between 0 and 1, the lines cross off the ends of both segments.
- If `t` or `u` is exactly 0 or 1, the segments just touch at an endpoint.

Try to make each of these cases happen in the parametric playground above. The
`t` and `u` readouts tell you which case you're in.

This makes for a much more useful function than one that returns a point or
nothing. It returns `t` and `u`, and the caller decides which case it cares
about. A hit test wants both values between 0 and 1. If you treat the first
segment as a ray, you'd want `t >= 0` instead. Here it is in a sketch, with one
endpoint following the mouse:

<Ask id="geometry-segment-intersection" mode="interacted" title="Intersecting segments">

Move the mouse around to swing the second segment. The function returns `null`
for parallel lines; otherwise, the sketch only draws the dot if both `t` and `u`
are between 0 and 1. Try changing that check to only look at `t`.

```p5exercise size="medium" autorun
let a, b, c

function setup() {
  createCanvas(400, 300)
  a = createVector(50, 250)
  b = createVector(350, 80)
  c = createVector(100, 40)
}

function draw() {
  background(240)
  const d = createVector(mouseX, mouseY)

  strokeWeight(3)
  stroke("steelblue")
  line(a.x, a.y, b.x, b.y)
  stroke("seagreen")
  line(c.x, c.y, d.x, d.y)

  const hit = intersectSegments(a, b, c, d)
  if (hit !== null && hit.t >= 0 && hit.t <= 1 && hit.u >= 0 && hit.u <= 1) {
    noStroke()
    fill("crimson")
    circle(hit.point.x, hit.point.y, 14)
  }
}

// Returns { t, u, point } for where the lines through AB and CD cross, or
// null if they're parallel. The caller decides which values of t and u count.
function intersectSegments(a, b, c, d) {
  const r = p5.Vector.sub(b, a)
  const s = p5.Vector.sub(d, c)
  const ca = p5.Vector.sub(c, a)
  const denom = r.cross(s).z
  if (abs(denom) < 0.000001) {
    return null
  }
  const t = ca.cross(s).z / denom
  const u = ca.cross(r).z / denom
  return { t, u, point: p5.Vector.lerp(a, b, t) }
}
```

</Ask>

Notice that the parallel check doesn't compare `denom` to exactly zero. Computer
arithmetic with fractional numbers is a tiny bit imprecise, so two lines that
_should_ be parallel might produce a cross product of 0.0000000001. Comparing
against a small tolerance instead of exactly zero is a common habit in geometry
code.

## Curves and splines

Straight segments only get you so far. p5 has two built-in families of smooth
curves: _Bézier curves_ (`bezier()` and `bezierVertex()`) and _cardinal splines_
(`spline()` and `splineVertex()`). If you've used the pen tool in a drawing
program like Illustrator, Figma, or Inkscape, you've already made Bézier curves.

These can feel like magic, but they aren't. Both are parametric, just like the
segments above, and both are built out of lerp.

<Callout intent="note">

This book uses version 2 of p5, where the cardinal spline functions are called
`spline()` and `splineVertex()`. Many tutorials online were written for older
versions, which called them `curve()` and `curveVertex()`.

</Callout>

### Bézier curves

A cubic Bézier curve is defined by four points. The two at the ends are called
_anchors_, and the curve always passes through them. The two in the middle are
called _handles_ (or control points), and the curve generally doesn't pass
through them. Like a segment, the curve is parametric: at `t = 0` it's at the
first anchor, and at `t = 1` it's at the last.

So how do you find the point at some other `t`? The answer is a recipe called
_de Casteljau's algorithm_, and all it does is lerp, over and over:

1. Lerp between each pair of neighboring points (P0 to P1, P1 to P2, P2 to P3)
   at the same `t`. That gives you three new points.
2. Lerp between neighboring pairs of _those_ three points, at the same `t`. That
   gives you two points.
3. Lerp between those two points. That last point is on the curve.

Sweep `t` from 0 to 1 and that last point traces out the whole curve. It's
segments within segments. Drag the `t` slider below to watch the construction.
The readout's color swatches match the first round of lerp, the second round,
and the point B(t) on the curve.

<CurvePlayground />

Now drag the handles. The dashed lines from each anchor to its handle show
something important: the curve leaves P0 heading straight toward P1, and it
arrives at P3 coming from the direction of P2. In other words, the handles set
the curve's _tangent_ (its direction) at each end. The farther out a handle is,
the longer the curve keeps heading in that direction before it bends away.

There's a bonus here, too: the line from the second round of lerp is always
tangent to the curve at B(t). The final lerp moves along that line, so that's
the direction the curve is going.

Two more facts worth knowing:

- The whole curve stays inside the polygon made by its four points (its _convex
  hull_). That's handy for a quick "is the mouse anywhere near this curve?"
  check.
- If you multiply out all three rounds of lerp, you get a polynomial in `t`:
  $B(t) = (1-t)^3 P_0 + 3(1-t)^2 t\,P_1 + 3(1-t)t^2 P_2 + t^3 P_3$. You'll see
  this formula in other places, but it's the same thing as the construction you
  just played with.

In p5, `bezier(x0, y0, x1, y1, x2, y2, x3, y3)` draws one curve. The playground
shows the call for the curve on its canvas. To make a longer path out of several
curves, start with `vertex()` for the first anchor inside `beginShape()`, then
call `bezierVertex()` three times per curve: handle, handle, anchor. p5 also has
`bezierPoint()` and `bezierTangent()` for finding the point and direction at a
given `t`, if you need them for something other than drawing.

### Cardinal splines

A cardinal spline is defined by a list of points, and the curve passes through
every one of them. There are no handles off to the side.

How does it decide which direction to go at each point? It looks at the point's
two neighbors. The tangent at a point is parallel to the line between the point
before it and the point after it. Hover over a point in the playground below to
see this: the dashed line connects its neighbors, and the solid line through the
point is parallel to it.

<CurvePlayground mode="cardinal" />

The _tightness_ setting scales how strong that tangent is. At 0 (p5's default)
you get the popular _Catmull-Rom_ spline. As tightness goes up, the curve gets
pulled tighter between the points, until at 1 it's just straight segments.

Because each piece of the curve depends on the points on either side of it, the
first and last points need special treatment: each has only one neighbor. p5
gives you two choices with `splineProperty("ends", ...)`. With `INCLUDE` (the
default), the curve goes all the way through the first and last points. With
`EXCLUDE`, those two points only steer the curve's direction, and the curve
starts and ends at their neighbors instead. Try both.

Now check the "Show Bézier handles" box. Here's the secret: every piece of a
cardinal spline is a cubic Bézier curve in disguise. p5 computes the handles for
you from the neighboring points, and then draws ordinary Bézier curves.

### Control and continuity

So which kind of curve should you use? It depends on what kind of control you
want.

With a Bézier curve, the handles are off the curve, which can feel indirect at
first. But they give you precise control over the direction of the curve at each
end, and how strongly it heads that way. That's why drawing programs use them.

With a cardinal spline, what you click is what you get, since every point is on
the curve. But you can't set the direction at a point yourself; the neighbors
decide it. Moving one point also bends the pieces of curve next to it. And
sometimes the curve overshoots, swinging wide between two points.

The other big difference shows up when you chain curves together. Does the joint
between two pieces look smooth? There are a few levels of smooth:

- **Connected:** there's no gap. One piece starts where the last one ended.
- **Same direction:** there's no corner. The curve heads the same way on both
  sides of the joint.
- **Same speed:** the curve is also changing at the same rate on both sides,
  which makes motion along it look even smoother.

(Graphics people call these $C^0$, $G^1$, and $C^1$ continuity, if you want to
look them up.)

Go back to the Bézier playground and check "Chain two curves." Chained Béziers
are connected as long as each one starts at the last one's ending anchor. But
they only meet without a corner if the handles on either side of the joint line
up, pointing in opposite directions. That's your job, not p5's. With "Smooth
joint" checked, the playground does it for you: drag one of the handles next to
the joint, and the other one swings around to match. Uncheck it, and you can
make a sharp corner. Drawing programs make the same distinction between "smooth"
and "corner" points.

A cardinal spline is smooth through every point automatically, since the tangent
at each point comes from the same pair of neighbors on both sides. You get
smoothness for free. The trade-off is that you can't make a sharp corner without
tricks, like putting two points right on top of each other.

## Hit tests

A _hit test_ asks whether two primitives intersect. Usually one of them is a
point, often the mouse, so the question becomes "is this point inside this
shape?"

**Point in circle:** a point is inside a circle if its distance from the center
is less than the radius.

```js
dist(p.x, p.y, center.x, center.y) <= radius
```

**Point in rectangle:** a rectangle whose sides line up with the x and y axes is
called an _axis-aligned bounding box_, or AABB. A point is inside it if it's
between the left and right sides, and between the top and bottom. That's four
comparisons. AABBs show up all over the place, because they're so cheap to test.
You'll see them again in the data structures chapter.

within a few pixels.

**Point in polygon:** this one is clever. From the point, cast a ray in any
direction, and count how many of the polygon's edges it crosses. If it crosses
an odd number of edges, the point is inside. If it crosses an even number
(including zero), the point is outside. Picture it: every time the ray crosses
an edge, it goes from inside to outside or the other way around, and it always
ends up outside, since the polygon doesn't go on forever. This is called the
_even-odd rule_, and it works even for polygons with dents in them.

Here are all four tests in one sketch. The polygon test uses a ray pointing to
the right, which makes the math simple: for each edge that spans the point's y
coordinate, find the x where the edge crosses that height, and count it if it's
to the right of the point. The sketch flips an `inside` boolean at each
crossing. You could just as well count the crossings and check
`count % 2 === 1` at the end; it's the same test.

  circleCenter = createVector(90, 90)
  box = { x: 200, y: 40, w: 140, h: 80 }
  segStart = createVector(40, 190)
  segEnd = createVector(170, 270)
  polygon = [
    createVector(230, 160),
    createVector(370, 170),
    createVector(340, 280),
    createVector(295, 210),
    createVector(240, 270),
  ]

function draw() {
  background(240)
  const mouse = createVector(mouseX, mouseY)
  strokeWeight(2)
  stroke(0)

  fill(pointInCircle(mouse, circleCenter, 50) ? "orange" : "white")
  circle(circleCenter.x, circleCenter.y, 100)

  fill(pointInRect(mouse, box) ? "orange" : "white")
  rect(box.x, box.y, box.w, box.h)

  fill(pointInPolygon(mouse, polygon) ? "orange" : "white")
  beginShape()
  for (const p of polygon) {
    vertex(p.x, p.y)
  }
  endShape(CLOSE)

  line(segStart.x, segStart.y, segEnd.x, segEnd.y)

function pointInCircle(p, center, radius) {
  return dist(p.x, p.y, center.x, center.y) <= radius
}

function pointInRect(p, r) {
  return p.x >= r.x && p.x <= r.x + r.w && p.y >= r.y && p.y <= r.y + r.h
}

function pointNearSegment(p, a, b, tolerance) {
  const r = p5.Vector.sub(b, a)
  const t = constrain(p5.Vector.sub(p, a).dot(r) / r.dot(r), 0, 1)
  const closest = p5.Vector.lerp(a, b, t)
  return dist(p.x, p.y, closest.x, closest.y) <= tolerance
}

// Cast a ray from p to the right and count the edges it crosses.
function pointInPolygon(p, corners) {
  let inside = false
  for (let i = 0; i < corners.length; i++) {
    const a = corners[i]
    const b = corners[(i + 1) % corners.length]
    // does this edge span the ray's height?
    if (a.y > p.y !== b.y > p.y) {
      // the x where the edge crosses that height
      const crossX = a.x + ((p.y - a.y) / (b.y - a.y)) * (b.x - a.x)
      if (crossX > p.x) {
        inside = !inside
      }
    }
  }
  return inside
}

All of these tests have something in common: they assume the point and the shape
are measured in the same coordinate system. But in a real program, shapes often
move, turn, and change size. A spaceship that's been moved, rotated, and scaled
draws itself in its own coordinates, not the canvas's. To hit-test it, we need a
way to move between coordinate systems. That's what affine transforms are for.

## Affine transforms

So far, we've placed every shape using the canvas's coordinates, which we'll
call _world_ coordinates. But anything you draw can have its own local
coordinate system. Affine transforms let a drawing live in its own frame of
reference, and then get placed into the world wherever you want it.

### Drawing relative to your own origin

In the Space Junk homework, you'll fly a ship called the Magpie, and it gets
placed in the world using affine transforms. The Magpie knows how to draw itself
around its own `(0, 0)`, which sits right in the middle of its hull. The
platform, the window, and the tractor beam are all drawn relative to that
origin.

This is a big deal. The drawing code doesn't need to know or care where the ship
is, which way it's facing, or how big it is. It just does its thing in its own
frame of reference, and something else decides where that frame goes in the
world. That's a serious cognitive win. It's also laziness in the good,
programmer-virtue sense: you write the drawing code once, in the simplest
possible coordinates, and reuse it everywhere.

### The three common transforms

There are three transforms you'll use all the time:

- **Translate** moves the coordinate system by some amount in x and y.
- **Rotate** spins the coordinate system around its origin by some angle, in
  radians.
- **Scale** stretches or squishes the coordinate system, either by the same
  amount in both directions, or by different amounts in x and y.

There are others, like _skew_ (also called _shear_), which slants things
sideways. But these three cover nearly everything you'll need.

One thing to watch out for: since y points down on the canvas, a positive
rotation looks _clockwise_, not counterclockwise like in a math class.

### The current matrix in p5

p5, like most graphics libraries, keeps track of a "current" transform that
applies to everything you draw. Calling `translate(dx, dy)`, `rotate(angle)`,
`scale(s)`, or `scale(sx, sy)` doesn't replace the current transform. It
combines the new one with whatever is already there.

The current transform works with `push()` and `pop()`, just like fill and stroke
colors do. `push()` saves the current transform, and `pop()` throws away every
change made since the matching `push()`. This is how you keep one object's
transforms from leaking into the next.

`push()` and `pop()`, each one starts fresh from the untransformed canvas.

### Order matters

Transforms happen in sequence, and each one works in the coordinate system the
previous ones left behind. So translating and then rotating is _not_ the same as
rotating and then translating. If you rotate first, the x axis now points in a
different direction, so a translate afterward moves along that new direction.

A good recipe for placing an object in the world:

1. Translate to where it goes.
2. Rotate it to face the right way.
3. Scale it to the right size.
4. Draw it at `(0, 0)`.

### Under the hood: matrices

Behind the scenes, each transform is a grid of numbers called a _matrix_. In 2D,
it's a 3x3 matrix, and multiplying a point by it moves the point from one
coordinate system into another. Each of the transforms has its own matrix shape,
where $t_x$ and $t_y$ are the translation amounts, $s_x$ and $s_y$ are the scale
factors, and $\theta$ is the rotation angle:

$$
\text{Identity} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\text{Translate} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & t_x \\ 0 & 1 & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\text{Scale} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & s_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\text{Rotate} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

You'll rarely write these by hand in p5, since `translate`, `rotate`, and
`scale` build them for you. But it's good to know they're there.

The _identity_ matrix is the "do nothing" transform: multiplying a point by it
gives you back the same point. It's what the current matrix starts out as, and
it's what an empty stack means in the playground above.

Why are these called _affine_ transforms? The name goes back to the
mathematician Leonhard Euler, who in 1748 used the Latin word _affinis_, meaning
"related" (the same root as the English word _affinity_). Two shapes are
affine-related if an affine transform turns one into the other. These
transforms can move, turn, and stretch things, but some things always survive:
straight lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, and a point halfway
along a segment stays halfway along it. So shapes that look quite different can
still be relatives. A square and any parallelogram are related this way, and so
are a circle and any ellipse.

Here's the part that makes it all work: multiplying two 3x3 matrices together
gives you another 3x3 matrix. So any chain of transforms, no matter how long,
collapses into a single matrix. That's what p5's current matrix is: every
transform you've applied so far this frame, combined into one. (`push()` doesn't
reset it. It just records the current matrix, along with other settings like
fill and stroke, so that `pop()` can restore them later.)

In 3D graphics, the same idea uses 4x4 matrices. Your computer's graphics card
(GPU) is built to multiply huge numbers of these very, very quickly.
