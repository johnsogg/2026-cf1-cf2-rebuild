### 1b. Wicked problems (pp. 63-65)

**p.63**
- "This is a good time to mention wicked problems. Could do a whole talk on
  this, but briefly, we can list out the characteristics of wicked problems, as
  all design problems share at least some of these characteristics & sometimes
  all of them."
- The list, with the short gloss in parentheses:
  1. No definitive formulation (problem set undefined)
  2. No stopping rule (never done)
  3. Not right or wrong, just better or worse (non-binary)
  4. No immediate/ultimate test of a solution (no definitive test)
  5. All solutions are one-shot; no opportunity to trial & error (no trial &
     error)
  6. Can't enumerate all possible solutions or operations (solution set
     undefined)
  7. All wicked problems unique (one of a kind)
  8. & they are all symptoms of other wicked problems (symptoms)
  9. Problem framing implies the nature of a solution (framing begets
     solution)
  10. Planner has no right to be wrong (catch-22 consequences)
- Footnote: "Not all design problems are wicked!" with a tame-to-wicked
  spectrum line.
- (draw) Left page (author's own placement, confirmed): the "you are here" diagram with the
  ten criteria numbered onto it: 1 at the pin; 7, 8, 9, 10 along the path;
  2, 3, 4, 5, 6 clustered at the X. In the talk the author relates each
  criterion to the part of the map it belongs to.

**p.65**
- *Left page (metadata):* "wicked problem as a framework for understanding the
  nature of design work"; "practical headaches: management, colleagues,
  stakeholders".
- "Bringing up wicked problems is useful because it gives us a vocabulary &
  framework for thinking about the breadth of challenges we face when doing
  design. And the simple three part diagram helps structure it."
- "When designing you will have other stakeholders who do not really understand
  the vast problem that practitioners face, and so if (say) your boss doesn't
  get that the starting condition is unclear, or the intended solution is
  undesired by social groups they didn't consider, etc etc; then you not only
  have the already challenging job of designing, but also the added stress of an
  authority figure demanding results, unreasonably. ((Insult to injury))"
- "There are many reasons why a design problem can go sideways, and many of them
  are social, not because of anything as convenient as 'other people dumb', but
  because we hold different knowledge, different understanding, different
  interests, different values, different motivations."

## Part 2: The job itself

What you do day to day; navigating uncertainty and social situations. (pp. 67-69)

**p.67**
- *Left page (metadata):* "more 'how to do the job' - tactics for social
  cohesion while getting the job done."
- "The job of designing is largely around navigating these social situations:
  clients, colleagues, management, all the while there are technical and
  economic unknowns and constraints. It is deeply rewarding when it works, when
  you ship something (or build or enact, or whatever verb you want) and land
  it."
- "But getting there is 99% of the job, and it can be fraught. And in navigating
  the social situation, your own thoughts & actions can fight against you."
- "QUALITY vs. QUANTITY isn't really apt. Getting the right design ->
  getting the design right."
- "perfectionism can fight you if your framing is imperfect (and it is!)"
- "You have to know when to relent, and not engage in 'pixel-fucking' as a boss
  I once had put it." **Slide: no profanity on screen. Say it aloud in class.**
- "You also need to know when to go wide (get ideas, frame the problem) and when
  to go deep (focus on a solution and detail it) and when to get feedback
  (objective and subjective are both good), and there is an art to taking
  feedback & incorporating it usefully."

**p.69**
- *Left page (metadata):* "more on doing the job"
- "If you can articulate and defend an argument around the parts [(draw) map
  labeled 1, 3, 2] 1. Current reality, 2. Desired reality, 3. Path for getting
  there ... and get agreement & buy in with the stakeholders, you're doing
  great."
- "As you walk the path, new information will come up, and you and everybody
  else had better pay attention and adjust. Maybe you notice the starting point
  was misunderstood, so you framed the problem wrong, so you're building the
  wrong thing."
- "Well... you were *already* building the wrong thing, and this new information
  just helps you understand. But what do you do about it? Tweak the design &
  keep going? Light it on fire & go back to the drawing board? Have shouting
  matches with your colleagues over who was wrong? Or, languish in meetings
  while avoiding the conversation?"
- "Pretty sure is a Dr. Seuss book on just this topic..."
- "Suffice it to say, design is a lot of social work, & not just pushing pixels
  all day."

## Part 3: How to get a job in the design profession

A favor to students who asked what to do with this degree. (pp. 71-73)

**p.71**
- *Left page, p.70 (sketch while thinking, not slide content):* brainstorm of
  the stages of the job-getting process: [work process for getting a job],
  [introductions], [lots of people], [get an interview], [good enough
  portfolio], [do good, defendable work], [impress your future colleagues],
  [references]. Idea for a graphic: a progressive reveal of what the process
  looks like from the outside, then a "Scooby-Doo reveal" that there's more to
  it than you'd first think. The stages are still to be worked out.
- "So if you still want to work as a designer, this is what you should be good
  at: understanding the current reality, framing the problem, understanding
  people (everyone, not just the users!), planning the solution, and being
  prepared for bumps on the path. And yes, you will push pixels, but the job is
  mostly people."
- "To get a job as a designer, the best way is to know somebody who is hiring, or
  know somebody who knows somebody. And I don't mean your LinkedIn network
  (though that is important too...). I mean people who would go to bat for you."
- "And that means (a) working with a lot of different people, and (b) having
  done good work so they will remember & go to bat!"

**p.73**
- "Carpet bombing your resume is a waste of time. Everybody does it, and hiring
  managers get overwhelmed so they get lazy: 'who do I know?' and ask on
  socials. (that is where LI helps.)"
- "So when your former classmate/colleague sees this, they think of you. Why
  would they think of you? And you get an intro. Either say yes or no. Have
  follow-through. If you ghost them, whoever did the intro also looks bad. This
  has been done, so you can do damage to yourself by flaking out."
- "Hiring managers want to know who you are, and what you can do and what your
  history has been, and what you'd add to the team. Your resume and portfolio
  will help them quickly get to a 'No' or 'maybe', because even a 15 minute
  screening call is a hassle."
- Starred: "Important: resume/portfolio is your AABB. It must be good to get you
  an interview, your interviews will likely look closer, but this is still
  superficial."
