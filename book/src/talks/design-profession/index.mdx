## Wicked problems

<WickedCriteria />

<Notes>

We can list out the characteristics of wicked problems, as all design problems
share at least some of these characteristics & sometimes all of them.

1. No definitive formulation
2. No stopping rule (never done)
3. Not right or wrong, just better or worse (non-binary)
4. No immediate/ultimate test of a solution (no definitive test)
5. All solutions are one-shot; no opportunity to trial & error (no trial & error)
6. Can't enumerate all possible solutions or operations (solution set undefined)
7. All wicked problems unique (one of a kind)
8. & they are all symptoms of other wicked problems (symptoms)
9. Problem framing implies the nature of a solution (framing begets solution)
10. Planner has no right to be wrong (catch-22 consequences)

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

<YouAreHere numbers label={false} />

<p style={{ fontSize: 40, textAlign: "center", marginTop: 20 }}>
  Not all design problems are wicked. Some are “tame.”
</p>

<Notes>

Not all design problems are wicked! By discussing wicked problems, I'm
introducing the idea of having a framework for thinking about what characterizes
a design problem.

Wicked problems are a formulation of the most extreme type of design problem:
the ones that are intractable.

Some design problems are, quote, tame. That sets up a nice binary: problems sit
somewhere on the tame-to-wicked spectrum.

Loose mapping of the criteria onto the map: the starting condition, the desired
ending condition, the approach. Some could arguably go in any of the three.

</Notes>

## Why problems go sideways

Mostly social.

<Reveal>

Different knowledge

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Different understanding

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Different interests

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Different values

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Different motivations

</Reveal>

<Notes>

There are many reasons why a design problem can go sideways, and many of them
are social, not because of anything as convenient as "other people dumb", but
because we hold different knowledge, different understanding, different
interests, different values, different motivations.

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

## No one stakeholder sees the full picture

<Reveal>

- The starting condition is unclear
- The solution is unwanted by groups nobody considered

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

The people writing the checks demand results.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

((Insult to injury))

</Reveal>

<Notes>

When designing you will have other stakeholders who do not really understand the
vast problem that practitioners face, and so if (say) your boss doesn't get that
the starting condition is unclear, or the intended solution is undesired by
social groups they didn't consider, etc etc; then you not only have the already
challenging job of designing, but also the added stress of an authority figure
demanding results, unreasonably. ((Insult to injury))

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

## The job is largely social

<Reveal>

Clients. Colleagues. Management.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Under it all: technical and economic unknowns and constraints.

</Reveal>

<Notes>

The job of designing is largely around navigating these social situations:
clients, colleagues, management, all the while there are technical and economic
unknowns and constraints.

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Shipping is fantastic

But: getting there is 99% of the job.

<Notes>

It is deeply rewarding when it works, when you ship something (or build or
enact, or whatever verb you want) and land it.

But getting there is 99% of the job, and it can be fraught. And in navigating
the social situation, your own thoughts & actions can fight against you.

</Notes>

</Slide>

## The art of feedback

<Reveal>

Taking it, and using it well.

## Quantitative: useful, but suspect

<Reveal>

The right questions, asked the right way, at the right time.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Collected reliably, without threats to validity.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Scrubbed and analyzed with appropriate mathematical modeling.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

In most business contexts: rare, and hard to pull off.

</Reveal>

<Notes>

There has been an over-reliance on quantitative, data-driven design.
Quantitative metrics are only effective if you can ask the right questions in
the right way at the right time and collect it reliably without threats to
validity. The data also has to be scrubbed and analyzed using appropriate
mathematical modeling. In most business contexts, this is rare, and difficult to
pull off.

So quantitative feedback, while extremely useful, should also be held with a
level of suspicion.

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Qualitative: biased by the source

<Reveal>

Biased by whoever is giving the feedback.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

A similar set of threats.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Caution here too.

</Reveal>

<Notes>

On the flip side, qualitative feedback can be highly biased by whoever is giving
the feedback. There is a similar set of threats to this mode of critique, and
caution should be used here as well.

</Notes>

</Slide>

## New information

<Reveal>

You agreed on the plan.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

New information arrives as you walk the path.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

Maybe the starting point was misunderstood.

</Reveal>

<Notes>

If you can articulate and defend an argument around the parts (1. current
reality, 2. desired reality, 3. path for getting there) ... and get agreement &
buy in with the stakeholders, you're doing great.

As you walk the path, new information will come up, and you and everybody else
had better pay attention and adjust. Maybe you notice the starting point was
misunderstood, so you framed the problem wrong, so you're building the wrong
thing.

</Notes>

<Reveal>

What do you do?

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

<Reveal>

<Reveal>

<Notes>

Well... you were _already_ building the wrong thing, and this new information

going? Light it on fire & go back to the drawing board? Have shouting matches
with your colleagues over who was wrong? Or, languish in meetings while avoiding
the conversation?

Pretty sure there's a Dr. Seuss book on just this topic...

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Design is social work

Not just pushing pixels all day.

<Reveal>

Don't expect to create nonstop. That's not the job.

</Reveal>

<Notes>

Suffice it to say, design is a lot of social work, & not just pushing pixels all
day.

And if you were under the impression that you'd get to create nonstop, that is
not the job. My goal here is to calibrate you on what the job is, so you can set
your expectations.

</Notes>

## Don't carpet-bomb your resume

<Reveal>

- Too many people do it.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- Hiring managers get overwhelmed, then lazy: "Who do I know?"

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- They ask on social media.

</Reveal>

<Notes>

Carpet bombing your resume is a waste of time. Everybody does it, and hiring
managers get overwhelmed so they get lazy: "who do I know?" and ask on socials.
(That is where LinkedIn helps.)

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Follow through on introductions

<Reveal>

- Someone thinks of you, and makes an intro.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- Say yes or no.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- Don't ignore the intro: respond to it definitively.

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- Ghosting makes them look bad, too.

</Reveal>

<Notes>

So when your former classmate or colleague sees this, they think of you. Why
would they think of you? And you get an intro. Either say yes or no. Have
follow-through. If you ghost them, whoever did the intro also looks bad. This
has been done, so you can do damage to yourself by flaking out.

</Notes>

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Resume & portfolio = foot in the door

<Reveal>

- For the hiring manager, your portfolio tells them:

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- Who you are, what you can do, where you've been

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- What you'd add to the team

</Reveal>

<Reveal>

- It helps them get to a quick _No_ or _Maybe_

</Reveal>

<Notes>

Hiring managers want to know who you are, and what you can do, and what your
history has been, and what you'd add to the team. Your resume and portfolio will
help them quickly get to a "No" or "maybe", because even a 15 minute screening
call is a hassle.

Important: your resume and portfolio are your AABB. They must be good enough to
get you an interview. Your interviews will likely look closer, but this is still
superficial.

</Notes>

</Slide>
